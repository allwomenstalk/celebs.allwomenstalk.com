# Vanity Fair’s Taylor Swift Profile and the Regina George Quote, Explained

Vanity Fair published a profile widely identified as being about Taylor Swift on November 23, 2026. May Jeong’s [“The Mastermind”](https://www.vanityfair.com/story/the-mastermind) calls its subject “The Star” throughout. The line that traveled furthest, “It’s truly Regina George,” came from an unnamed music-industry source. Vanity Fair says Swift declined to answer questions during reporting.

The profile tells two versions of the same rise to fame. One draws on the familiar story of a country singer who became a global pop star. The other revisits that history through people who remember her differently or take a more skeptical view of her public image. The [high-school accounts](#high-school) illustrate how much can turn on who is telling the story. The coverage of [Swift’s response](#response) offers another example of how easily an attributed remark can become a headline about something she supposedly said herself.

## What Vanity Fair published

May Jeong wrote the profile, and Hugo Yu is credited for the photographs. The headline on the page is “The Mastermind,” while the browser title asks what readers would think if they were told she was a mastermind. Jeong divides the story into A and B sides, like a record. The A side follows the familiar account of her rise. The B side revisits moments from that account through former classmates, people in the music business and other sources.

Vanity Fair says Jeong spoke to more than 100 sources. That number describes the magazine’s reporting process. The published piece lets readers see a smaller set of accounts in detail, including people who speak under their own names and people whose identities the magazine withholds. The strength of a particular claim depends on what the source says they saw, how specifically they describe it and whether another account supports it.

The profile gives other people aliases too. [Variety’s Chris Willman](https://variety.com/2026/music/columns/taylor-swift-vanity-fair-hit-piece-journalism-1236956512/) identifies “Redwood” as Travis Kelce and “The Brat” as Charli xcx. Swift’s career and relationships supply the clues that make the profile legible even with her name missing. Jeong also invites readers to look for a hidden message in the text, making that guessing game part of the reading experience.

## Why “Mastermind” sounds familiar

“Mastermind” closes the standard edition of Swift’s 2022 album _Midnights_. In the song, the narrator admits how deliberately she set up a romance that could have looked like chance. Vanity Fair’s longer headline appears to echo the song’s opening question in its chorus. The profile leaves that connection implicit and uses the idea of a mastermind to ask how much of a public persona someone can shape.

There is a second musical reference, according to [Page Six](https://pagesix.com/2026/11/23/entertainment/why-taylor-swift-isnt-losing-sleep-over-scathing-vanity-fair-expose-as-travis-kelce-stands-by-her-side/). The outlet reported that the odd capital letters scattered through the piece spell out “Look What You Made Me Do,” the title of a song from _Reputation_. Vanity Fair itself tells readers to look for an Easter egg.

## Who made the Regina George comparison?

It was “The Exec,” Jeong’s alias for a music-industry professional whose name the profile withholds. The remark appears in the B side after a passage about Swift’s relationships with other women in pop. The comparison invokes Rachel McAdams’s character in [_Mean Girls_](https://teen.allwomenstalk.com/mean-girls-references-you-need-to-be-aware-of-for-any-conversation/), whose power at school rests on who gets to belong to her circle.

Attribution matters here. The Regina George line gives readers one anonymous source’s interpretation of how Swift operates. The named classmates in the same article offer their own recollections. If you saw the comparison attached to a headline about Swift’s school days, those are two different pieces of reporting.

## What classmates and a teacher remember

Chanice Skinner, a former classmate, remembers Swift as part of the popular crowd and recalls feeling that people outside her friend group went unnoticed. Arielle Peppers, another former student, recalls Swift having Ugg boots in several colors. It’s a small detail about the social world of their school, and it became one of the most widely discussed anecdotes in the piece.

The lunch-table accounts show where memories diverge. Jeong revisits Swift’s recollection, shared in an old Katie Couric interview, of friends moving away from her at lunch. Sharon Luyben, a former music teacher, remembers assigned seating in junior high and disputes a version of the lunchroom story. The article presents conflicting memories, and the public reporting leaves the particular incident unresolved.

A named recollection tells readers who remembers a moment and gives them some context for that person’s perspective. Elsewhere, a source called “The Person Familiar” describes how they believe Swift handles disagreements. The profile gives readers less information to assess that source’s relationship to Swift or the experiences behind the assessment. The distinction applies even when two accounts appear in the same paragraph.

## Why the profile drew criticism

Willman’s Variety column took issue with the decision to withhold Swift’s name and with how much the material on the page supported the story’s premise. He also questioned how the magazine’s reported interview count compared with the number of sources readers actually hear from. It’s possible for reporters to speak to many people they never quote. Willman’s question was what the accounts that made it into the article added up to.

[TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/vanity-fair-taylor-swift-expose-social-media-reactions/) covered the reaction to the Ugg boots anecdote. Some readers wondered why a teenager’s collection of shoes carried so much weight in a story about an adult’s character. Others were intrigued by how many people in the piece appeared under aliases. Both reactions turn on the same editorial choice about how much meaning to give a small detail or a source readers know little about.

## Has Taylor Swift responded?

Vanity Fair says Swift declined to comment after the magazine sent her questions. As of Tuesday morning, November 24, we found no on-the-record public statement from Swift, Kelce or a named representative about the profile.

Page Six quoted an unnamed source it described as close to Swift and a second unnamed source who discussed Kelce. The outlet said representatives for Swift and Kelce, along with Vanity Fair and Jeong, did not immediately respond to its requests for comment. The account of how Swift felt came from the person Page Six interviewed.

## Is it a cover story, and did Swift pose for the photos?

Vanity Fair refers to a print issue in the piece. The online story and its credits leave its status as a print cover story unconfirmed. The photographs are credited to Hugo Yu, and the captions describe custom jackets by Alixe of Bohemia as homages with references for readers to spot. Kris Jensen is credited as prop stylist. The published captions give no confirmation of a Swift portrait sitting.

## Why the friendship story keeps changing

Swift’s public story has had room for both the kid who felt left out and the star whose friendships became part of her image. Even [older coverage of her celebrity friendships](https://celebs.allwomenstalk.com/celebrities-who-are-great-friends-with-taylor-swift/) shows how readily a group of friends became a story about belonging that fans could follow. The same attention can give a falling-out the weight of a public event, even when outsiders know very little about what happened.

The word “mastermind” captures a similar shift. In a song, it can sound playful, a confession about wanting something enough to plan for it. In a piece about power, it invites readers to scrutinize the intention behind each move. The Regina George analogy goes further by giving that intention a familiar villain. It raises a question about how we talk about ambitious women, especially when the same attention to relationships and reputation can be described as skill or manipulation. You can ask that question while still reading the specific claims closely and holding each one to the evidence the reporting supplies.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who wrote Vanity Fair’s Taylor Swift profile?

Journalist May Jeong wrote “The Mastermind,” and Hugo Yu is credited for the photography. Vanity Fair published the story on November 23, 2026.

### Does the profile name Taylor Swift?

It refers to her as “The Star.” The career details and other references identify her, and subsequent coverage discusses the piece as a Taylor Swift profile.

### Where did the Regina George quote come from?

Vanity Fair attributes it to a music-industry source identified only as “The Exec.”

### Did Taylor Swift give an interview for the profile?

Vanity Fair says it sent its subject questions and that she declined to comment. The magazine cites earlier public interviews as part of its account.
